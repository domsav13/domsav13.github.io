Cherry Blossom Visual Odometry

Created on May 01, 2026

2026

In tihs project, 3D structures and camera pose (orientation and translation) are recovered from video footage.

Description of the Data

The camera data was obtained by mounting a GoPro Hero camera (4K/30FPS) to a head strap, and then placing the head strap on a running hat. While wearing the hat, the GoPro recorded about 50 minutes of footage from a 10K run in Washington DC during peak cherry blossom season. A 2.5 minute clip of the footage was chosen as the data for this project, which lights the Hains Point “loop” in southern DC. The data was then processed through the GoPro Quik app which stabilizes the video. Editing software was also used to crop the video such that only the upper horizon is included, and the resolution was reduced to 720p and 24 FPS.

Fig. 1: GoPro camera and head mount

Bootstrapping

The first step of visual odometry is to initialize the feature extraction problem between two nearby frames in the video. In this project, LightGlue was used as a deep neural network feature matcher to improve the visual odometry pipeline, because bootstrapping is the most fragile part of the process. Rather than relying on traditional tracking methods such as the Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi (KLT) tracker, LightGlue is applied to identify correspondences between distinctive image features using learned matching logic. This made the initial feature matches more robust to viewpoint change, blur, texture, variation, and the complex natural scenery present in the footage. Once those correspondences were established, they could be used to estimate the relative camera motion between the frames and triangulate an initial set of 3D landmarks, giving the visual odometry system a stronger starting map.

Fig. 2 shows the matches between the image pair used in bootstrapping. As shown, the keypoints are very distinct, covering outlines in the pavement, curb edges, leafy features, and tree outlines.

Fig. 2: The visual odometry initialization between two nearby frames at the beginning of the video, showing feature matching.

Visual Odometry

After initial matches are established, the rest of the visual odometry pipeline proceeds by tracking image features from frame to frame, estimating the camera’s motion from those tracked correspondences, and continuously updating the 3D map of the scene. In each new frame, previously observed keypoints are followed using the tracking stage, outlier correspondences are rejected so that bad matches do not corrupt the motion estimate, and the camera pose is recovered relative to the existing map. New landmarks are then triangulated when enough reliable observations are available, while older points that become inconsistent or poorly tracked are removed to maintain stability. In this way, the pipeline alternates between feature tracking, motion estimation, and map refinement, allowing the camera trajectory to be reconstructed over time as the video progresses.

The full trajectory is shown in the video below.

The results show that the pipeline is able to retain sufficient feature points such that the motion estimate does not fail or lead to inaccuracy. Despite the presence of moving objects and occlusions (certain feature points disappearing suddenly due to changes in camera field of view), the localization and mapping is robust for the original data. As shown in Fig. 3, the density of the feature points also correlates with the ground truth 3D scene, as dense regions resemble the trees in the scene.
